Section 1: The Absolute Basics — Why Do Claims "Develop"?

Imagine you run a construction company. On January 15, a worker breaks his leg on site. Here's what happens:

• Day 1: Accident happens. Nobody tells the insurer yet.
• Month 2: Worker files a claim. Insurer opens a file. Initial reserve: $50K.
• Month 6: Medical bills come in. Reserve revised to $120K.
• Month 12: Worker sues for long-term disability. Reserve revised to $400K.
• Year 3: Lawsuit settled. Final paid amount: $350K.

That claim went from $0 → $50K → $120K → $400K → $350K over 3 years. It developed over time. Now multiply that by thousands of claims. THAT is what loss triangles track.

The Core Problem Reserving Solves

IBNR = Ultimate Losses − Paid (or Incurred) to Date

• Ultimate Losses: The total amount we'll EVER pay for accidents in a given year (once everything is settled, years from now)
• Paid to Date: What we've actually written checks for so far
• IBNR: The "reserve" we must hold today for future payments on past accidents

Paid vs Incurred — why both matter:
• Paid triangle: Only actual checks. Objective, can't be manipulated. But very slow to develop (claims take years to pay).
• Incurred triangle: Paid + reserves. More responsive (adjuster updates reserves quickly). But subjective (different adjusters = different reserve opinions).

Most companies build BOTH triangles and compare. If they give very different answers, investigate why.

Section 2: The Loss Triangle — What It Looks Like

A loss triangle is just a table where:
• Rows = accident years (when things happened)
• Columns = development periods (how old the data is)
• Cells = cumulative claims as of that development period

It's called a "triangle" because recent years have fewer columns filled in (we haven't observed them develop yet).

Building a Triangle from Raw Data in Python

# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# BUILDING A LOSS TRIANGLE FROM RAW CLAIMS DATA
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

# ─── Your raw data looks like this: ─────────────────────
# Each row = one claim transaction (a payment or reserve change)
# Columns: claim_id, accident_date, payment_date, paid_amount

raw_claims = pd.read_csv("claims_transactions.csv")
raw_claims['accident_date'] = pd.to_datetime(raw_claims['accident_date'])
raw_claims['payment_date'] = pd.to_datetime(raw_claims['payment_date'])

# ─── Step 1: Derive Accident Year and Development Year ───
raw_claims['accident_year'] = raw_claims['accident_date'].dt.year
raw_claims['payment_year'] = raw_claims['payment_date'].dt.year
raw_claims['dev_year'] = raw_claims['payment_year'] - raw_claims['accident_year'] + 1
# dev_year = 1 means "first year of development"
# e.g., accident in 2020, payment in 2020 → dev_year = 1
# e.g., accident in 2020, payment in 2022 → dev_year = 3

# ─── Step 2: Sum payments by (accident_year, dev_year) ───
# This gives INCREMENTAL paid losses
incremental = raw_claims.groupby(
    ['accident_year', 'dev_year']
)['paid_amount'].sum().reset_index()

# ─── Step 3: Pivot into triangle shape ──────────────────
inc_triangle = incremental.pivot(
    index='accident_year',    # Rows = accident years
    columns='dev_year',       # Columns = development years
    values='paid_amount'      # Values = paid amounts
).fillna(0)

# ─── Step 4: Convert INCREMENTAL to CUMULATIVE ──────────
# Cumulative = running total across development years
cum_triangle = inc_triangle.cumsum(axis=1)
# axis=1 means "sum across columns (left to right)"
# So DY3 cumulative = DY1 + DY2 + DY3

print("CUMULATIVE PAID LOSS TRIANGLE ($000s):")
print((cum_triangle / 1000).round(0))
Incremental vs Cumulative:
• Incremental: How much was paid IN each development year (DY3 incremental = payments made in the 3rd year only)
• Cumulative: Total paid UP TO each development year (DY3 cumulative = DY1 + DY2 + DY3)

Chain Ladder works on the cumulative triangle. Always convert to cumulative before computing development factors.

Section 3: Age-to-Age Development Factors (Link Ratios)

A development factor answers: "For every $1 of claims at age 2, how much will there be at age 3?"

If the factor from DY2→DY3 is 1.18, it means claims at age 2 will grow by 18% by age 3. If you have $5.6M at DY2, you'll have $5.6M × 1.18 = $6.6M at DY3.

Computing Development Factors from the Triangle

Age-to-Age Factor (Link Ratio) from DY(n) to DY(n+1):

f(n→n+1) = Σ [C(i, n+1)] / Σ [C(i, n)]

Where C(i, n) = cumulative loss for accident year i at development year n.
We sum over ALL accident years that have both DY(n) and DY(n+1) observed.

Worked Example

What the factors tell you:
• Big early factors (1.595): Claims grow fast in the first years — claims being reported, initial payments made
• Shrinking factors (1.057, 1.025): Growth slows — most claims are known, just settling
• Factor → 1.000: The "tail" — claims are virtually fully developed

The "tail factor" (DY6→Ultimate = 1.000) means we assume no more development after year 6. This is a judgment call. Long-tail lines like asbestos or medical malpractice might need a factor > 1.000 even at year 20.

Cumulative Development Factor (CDF) — "How Much More Will It Grow?"

CDF to Ultimate from age n = product of all remaining factors:

CDF(1→Ult) = 1.595 × 1.182 × 1.057 × 1.025 × 1.008 × 1.000 = 2.043
CDF(2→Ult) = 1.182 × 1.057 × 1.025 × 1.008 × 1.000 = 1.281
CDF(3→Ult) = 1.057 × 1.025 × 1.008 × 1.000 = 1.092
CDF(4→Ult) = 1.025 × 1.008 × 1.000 = 1.033
CDF(5→Ult) = 1.008 × 1.000 = 1.008
CDF(6→Ult) = 1.000

Interpretation: Claims at DY1 will roughly DOUBLE by ultimate (CDF = 2.04). Claims at DY4 will only grow 3.3% more.

Python: Compute Development Factors

# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# COMPUTE AGE-TO-AGE DEVELOPMENT FACTORS
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

def compute_dev_factors(cum_triangle):
    """
    Compute weighted-average age-to-age development factors.
    
    Parameters:
    -----------
    cum_triangle : DataFrame with rows=AY, columns=DY, values=cumulative losses
    
    Returns:
    --------
    Series of development factors (DY1→2, DY2→3, etc.)
    """
    factors = {}
    dev_years = sorted(cum_triangle.columns)
    
    for i in range(len(dev_years) - 1):
        dy_current = dev_years[i]
        dy_next = dev_years[i + 1]
        
        # Get pairs where BOTH current and next period are observed (not NaN)
        mask = cum_triangle[dy_current].notna() & cum_triangle[dy_next].notna()
        
        # Weighted average: sum(next) / sum(current)
        numerator = cum_triangle.loc[mask, dy_next].sum()
        denominator = cum_triangle.loc[mask, dy_current].sum()
        
        factor = numerator / denominator
        factors[f"{dy_current}→{dy_next}"] = factor
    
    return pd.Series(factors)

# Run it:
factors = compute_dev_factors(cum_triangle)
print("AGE-TO-AGE FACTORS:")
print(factors.round(4))

# ─── Cumulative Development Factors ─────────────────────
def compute_cdf(factors):
    """Convert age-to-age factors to cumulative (to ultimate)."""
    cdfs = {}
    factor_values = list(factors.values)[::-1]  # Reverse order
    
    running_product = 1.0
    for i, f in enumerate(factor_values):
        running_product *= f
        cdfs[len(factor_values) - i] = running_product
    
    # Add the last development year (CDF = 1.0, already at ultimate)
    cdfs[len(factor_values) + 1] = 1.0
    
    return pd.Series(cdfs).sort_index()

cdfs = compute_cdf(factors)
print("\nCUMULATIVE DEVELOPMENT FACTORS (to ultimate):")
for dy, cdf in cdfs.items():
    pct_developed = 1 / cdf * 100
    print(f"  DY {dy}: CDF = {cdf:.4f} ({pct_developed:.1f}% developed)")
% Developed = 1 / CDF × 100. If CDF(DY1) = 2.043, then at DY1 claims are 1/2.043 = 48.9% developed. Meaning we've only seen about half the ultimate cost after 1 year. This is typical for GL/construction.

Section 4: Chain Ladder Method — The Foundation

Chain Ladder is dead simple:
1. Look at how claims developed in the PAST (compute development factors from older, fully-observed years).
2. Assume the FUTURE will develop the same way.
3. Multiply each incomplete year's latest value by the remaining CDF to get its ultimate.

That's it. The entire method is one multiplication.
Chain Ladder Ultimate = Latest Observed Value × CDF to Ultimate

For AY 2024 (at DY1): Ultimate = $4,800K × 2.043 = $9,807K
For AY 2023 (at DY2): Ultimate = $7,000K × 1.281 = $8,967K
For AY 2022 (at DY3): Ultimate = $7,700K × 1.092 = $8,408K

Chain Ladder in Python

# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# CHAIN LADDER METHOD — Complete Implementation
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

def chain_ladder(cum_triangle, tail_factor=1.0):
    """
    Compute Chain Ladder ultimate losses and IBNR.
    
    Parameters:
    -----------
    cum_triangle : DataFrame (rows=AY, columns=DY, values=cumulative losses)
    tail_factor : float, factor from last observed DY to ultimate (default 1.0)
    
    Returns:
    --------
    DataFrame with columns: AY, latest_observed, dev_year, cdf, ultimate, ibnr
    """
    
    # Step 1: Compute age-to-age factors
    dev_years = sorted(cum_triangle.columns)
    ata_factors = []
    
    for i in range(len(dev_years) - 1):
        col_now = dev_years[i]
        col_next = dev_years[i + 1]
        
        mask = cum_triangle[col_now].notna() & cum_triangle[col_next].notna()
        factor = cum_triangle.loc[mask, col_next].sum() / cum_triangle.loc[mask, col_now].sum()
        ata_factors.append(factor)
    
    # Step 2: Compute CDFs (from each DY to ultimate)
    # Include the tail factor
    ata_factors_with_tail = ata_factors + [tail_factor]
    
    cdfs = {}
    for dy_idx in range(len(dev_years)):
        # CDF = product of all remaining factors from this DY onward
        remaining = ata_factors_with_tail[dy_idx:]
        cdfs[dev_years[dy_idx]] = np.prod(remaining)
    
    # Step 3: For each AY, get latest observed and multiply by CDF
    results = []
    for ay in cum_triangle.index:
        # Find the latest non-NaN development year for this AY
        row = cum_triangle.loc[ay].dropna()
        if len(row) == 0:
            continue
        latest_dy = row.index[-1]
        latest_value = row.iloc[-1]
        
        cdf = cdfs[latest_dy]
        ultimate = latest_value * cdf
        ibnr = ultimate - latest_value
        
        results.append({
            'accident_year': ay,
            'latest_observed': latest_value,
            'at_dev_year': latest_dy,
            'cdf_to_ultimate': cdf,
            'ultimate': ultimate,
            'ibnr': ibnr
        })
    
    results_df = pd.DataFrame(results)
    
    # Print summary
    print("="*70)
    print("CHAIN LADDER RESULTS")
    print("="*70)
    print(results_df.to_string(index=False, float_format='{:,.0f}'.format))
    print(f"\nTotal IBNR Reserve: ${results_df['ibnr'].sum():,.0f}")
    print(f"Total Ultimate:     ${results_df['ultimate'].sum():,.0f}")
    print("="*70)
    
    return results_df

# Run it:
cl_results = chain_ladder(cum_triangle, tail_factor=1.00)

Chain Ladder Assumptions (& When They Fail)

The ONE critical assumption of Chain Ladder:
"Future development will follow the same pattern as past development."

This fails when:
• Claims handling procedures change (new team settles faster → factors drop)
• A legal ruling changes (class action makes old claims re-open → factors spike)
• Mix of business shifts (moved from short-tail to long-tail → factors increase)
• Inflation changes dramatically (claims paid later cost more in nominal terms)

Section 5: Bornhuetter-Ferguson (BF) Method

Chain Ladder has a problem: for the most recent year (only 1 year of development), it multiplies a small number by a large CDF, which is very sensitive to noise. If one large claim inflates DY1, Chain Ladder blows up the ultimate.

BF says: "Don't put all your trust in the latest data. Blend it with a prior estimate of how much the year SHOULD cost." The less developed the year is, the more we lean on the prior.

The BF Formula

BF Ultimate = Actual Paid + Expected Remaining

Where:
• Actual Paid = what we've observed so far (same as Chain Ladder)
• Expected Remaining = Prior × (1 − % Developed)
• Prior = independent estimate of ultimate (e.g., from pricing, or earned premium × expected loss ratio)
• % Developed = 1 / CDF

BF Ultimate = Paid + Prior × (1 − 1/CDF)

Worked Example

Say our prior estimate (from pricing) is that each year should cost about $8,500K ultimate:

AYPaidCDF% DevPriorExpected Remaining = Prior × (1 − %Dev)BF Ultimate

20217,6001.03396.8%8,5008,500 × 0.032 = 2727,872

Compare AY 2024:
• Chain Ladder: $4,800 × 2.043 = $9,807K (entirely driven by observed data)
• BF: $4,800 + $4,344 = $9,144K (blends observed with prior)

BF is lower because it doesn't let the (potentially noisy) DY1 observation fully dictate the ultimate. It anchors partially to the prior of $8,500K.

For mature years (2019, 2020), BF and Chain Ladder give almost the same answer because % developed is near 100%.

BF in Python

# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# BORNHUETTER-FERGUSON METHOD
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

def bornhuetter_ferguson(cum_triangle, prior_ultimates, cdfs):
    """
    Compute BF ultimate losses.
    
    Parameters:
    -----------
    cum_triangle : DataFrame (the loss triangle)
    prior_ultimates : dict or Series {AY: prior_ultimate_estimate}
                     Often = earned_premium * expected_loss_ratio
    cdfs : dict or Series {DY: CDF_to_ultimate}
    
    Returns:
    --------
    DataFrame with BF results
    """
    results = []
    
    for ay in cum_triangle.index:
        row = cum_triangle.loc[ay].dropna()
        if len(row) == 0:
            continue
            
        latest_dy = row.index[-1]
        latest_value = row.iloc[-1]
        
        cdf = cdfs[latest_dy]
        pct_developed = 1.0 / cdf  # How much of ultimate we've seen
        
        prior = prior_ultimates[ay]
        expected_remaining = prior * (1 - pct_developed)
        
        bf_ultimate = latest_value + expected_remaining
        bf_ibnr = bf_ultimate - latest_value
        
        results.append({
            'accident_year': ay,
            'paid_to_date': latest_value,
            'prior_ultimate': prior,
            'pct_developed': pct_developed,
            'expected_remaining': expected_remaining,
            'bf_ultimate': bf_ultimate,
            'bf_ibnr': bf_ibnr
        })
    
    return pd.DataFrame(results)

# ─── Set up priors ──────────────────────────────────────
# Common approach: Prior = Earned Premium × Expected Loss Ratio
earned_premium = {2019: 12000, 2020: 12500, 2021: 13000, 
                  2022: 13500, 2023: 14000, 2024: 15000}
expected_lr = 0.60  # 60% loss ratio (from pricing or industry benchmark)

prior_ultimates = {ay: prem * expected_lr for ay, prem in earned_premium.items()}
# e.g., 2024: $15,000K × 0.60 = $9,000K expected ultimate

bf_results = bornhuetter_ferguson(cum_triangle, prior_ultimates, cdfs)
print(bf_results.to_string(index=False))

When to Use BF vs Chain Ladder

✅ Use Chain Ladder when:

  • Years are mostly developed (>60%)
  • Development pattern is stable year to year
  • You trust the data (large volume, no unusual events)
  • No strong prior information available

✅ Use BF when:

  • Years are immature (<50% developed)
  • One large claim is distorting early development
  • New line of business (limited history)
  • You have a reliable prior (pricing, benchmarks)

Section 6: Cape Cod Method

Cape Cod is like BF, but instead of YOU choosing the prior, the DATA determines it. It calculates the "best fit" expected loss ratio from the triangle itself, then uses that as the prior in BF. It's a compromise between Chain Ladder (fully data-driven) and BF (partially judgment-driven).
Cape Cod Method:

Step 1: Compute the "implied" loss ratio from all years:
ELR = Σ (Latest Paid for each AY) / Σ (Earned Premium × % Developed for each AY)

Step 2: Use this ELR as the prior in the BF formula:
Cape Cod Ultimate = Paid + ELR × Premium × (1 − % Developed) 
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# CAPE COD METHOD
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

def cape_cod(cum_triangle, earned_premium, cdfs):
    """
    Cape Cod method: data-driven expected loss ratio + BF approach.
    """
    
    # Step 1: Compute the "used-up" premium for each AY
    # Used-up premium = Earned Premium × % Developed
    # This represents how much premium has been "earned" through development
    
    numerator = 0  # Sum of latest paid across all AYs
    denominator = 0  # Sum of (premium × % developed) across all AYs
    
    ay_data = []
    for ay in cum_triangle.index:
        row = cum_triangle.loc[ay].dropna()
        if len(row) == 0:
            continue
        latest_dy = row.index[-1]
        latest_value = row.iloc[-1]
        cdf = cdfs[latest_dy]
        pct_dev = 1.0 / cdf
        
        numerator += latest_value
        denominator += earned_premium[ay] * pct_dev
        
        ay_data.append({
            'ay': ay, 'paid': latest_value, 
            'premium': earned_premium[ay], 'pct_dev': pct_dev, 'cdf': cdf
        })
    
    # The implied Expected Loss Ratio
    elr = numerator / denominator
    print(f"Cape Cod Implied ELR: {elr:.3f} ({elr*100:.1f}%)")
    
    # Step 2: Apply BF formula with the computed ELR
    results = []
    for d in ay_data:
        prior = d['premium'] * elr  # Data-driven prior
        expected_remaining = prior * (1 - d['pct_dev'])
        cc_ultimate = d['paid'] + expected_remaining
        
        results.append({
            'accident_year': d['ay'],
            'paid': d['paid'],
            'elr_prior': prior,
            'cc_ultimate': cc_ultimate,
            'cc_ibnr': cc_ultimate - d['paid']
        })
    
    return pd.DataFrame(results), elr

cc_results, elr = cape_cod(cum_triangle, earned_premium, cdfs)
print(cc_results.to_string(index=False))

Before you trust any development factors, you need to check: "Is the development pattern stable, or is something weird happening?" Like a doctor checking vital signs before surgery.

• Stable factors (like above): Each column is consistent across rows → good! The development pattern is stable.
• Trend in factors: If f(1→2) is increasing each year (1.55, 1.58, 1.62, 1.65) → claims are taking longer to develop (maybe slower settlement). Investigate why.
• Outlier factor: If one year has f(1→2) = 2.3 when others are ~1.6 → one large claim or data issue. Investigate, maybe exclude that year from factor calculation.

Diagnostic 2: Calendar Year Effects

A "calendar year effect" means something happened at a specific POINT IN TIME that affected all accident years simultaneously. Example: In 2022, the company changed its claims team and settled old claims faster. This would show up as ALL accident years having unusually high development in 2022 (a diagonal pattern in the triangle). 
# ─── Diagnostic: Check for calendar year effects ─────────
# A calendar year effect shows up as a diagonal pattern

# Convert cumulative to incremental
inc_triangle = cum_triangle.diff(axis=1)
inc_triangle.iloc[:, 0] = cum_triangle.iloc[:, 0]  # First column stays

# For each diagonal (calendar year), compute the average development
# Calendar year = AY + DY - 1
def get_calendar_year_totals(inc_triangle):
    """Sum incremental payments by calendar year (diagonals)."""
    cal_year_totals = {}
    for ay in inc_triangle.index:
        for dy in inc_triangle.columns:
            if pd.notna(inc_triangle.loc[ay, dy]):
                cal_year = ay + dy - 1
                cal_year_totals[cal_year] = cal_year_totals.get(cal_year, 0) + inc_triangle.loc[ay, dy]
    return pd.Series(cal_year_totals).sort_index()

cal_totals = get_calendar_year_totals(inc_triangle)
print("Payments by Calendar Year:")
print(cal_totals)
# If one calendar year is unusually high → calendar year effect

# ─── Diagnostic: Plot individual factors vs weighted average ──
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def plot_factor_stability(cum_triangle):
    """Plot individual ATA factors to check for stability/trends."""
    dev_years = sorted(cum_triangle.columns)
    
    fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, len(dev_years)-1, figsize=(14, 4), sharey=False)
    
    for i, ax in enumerate(axes):
        col_now = dev_years[i]
        col_next = dev_years[i+1]
        
        mask = cum_triangle[col_now].notna() & cum_triangle[col_next].notna()
        factors = cum_triangle.loc[mask, col_next] / cum_triangle.loc[mask, col_now]
        
        # Weighted average
        weighted_avg = cum_triangle.loc[mask, col_next].sum() / cum_triangle.loc[mask, col_now].sum()
        
        ax.bar(range(len(factors)), factors.values, color='#2980b9', alpha=0.7)
        ax.axhline(weighted_avg, color='red', linestyle='--', linewidth=2)
        ax.set_title(f'f({col_now}→{col_next})\nAvg={weighted_avg:.3f}', fontsize=9)
        ax.set_xticks(range(len(factors)))
        ax.set_xticklabels([str(ay) for ay in factors.index], rotation=45, fontsize=7)
    
    plt.suptitle('Age-to-Age Factor Stability Check', fontweight='bold')
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.show()

plot_factor_stability(cum_triangle)

Top Interview Questions

Q: "What is IBNR?"

A: "IBNR = Ultimate Losses minus what we've already recorded (paid or incurred). It's the reserve we hold for: (1) claims that happened but haven't been reported yet, (2) reported claims that will develop further (payments not yet made). It ensures we have enough money set aside to pay future obligations from past events."

Q: "Explain Chain Ladder in one sentence."

A: "Chain Ladder multiplies the latest observed cumulative loss by a development factor derived from how older accident years matured, assuming the future will develop like the past."

Q: "When does Chain Ladder fail?"

A: "When the assumption of stable development patterns breaks: (1) claims handling changes (new team, new process), (2) legal environment shifts (new court rulings), (3) inflation changes, (4) mix of business changes. Also fails for immature years where one large claim can dominate the latest observation and get projected forward."

Q: "Why use BF instead of Chain Ladder?"

A: "BF is more stable for immature years because it blends observed data with a prior estimate. Chain Ladder is 100% data-driven — if DY1 is noisy (one large claim), CL multiplies that noise by a large CDF. BF limits the damage by anchoring partially to the prior. For mature years, they converge to the same answer."

Q: "Paid vs Incurred triangle — which is better?"

A: "Both have trade-offs. Paid is objective (actual cash out the door) but slow to develop. Incurred (paid + case reserves) responds faster but is subjective — different adjusters set different reserves. Best practice: run both, compare results. If they disagree significantly, investigate: are case reserves over/under-stated? Large discrepancies flag potential reserve inadequacy."

Q: "What is a tail factor?"

A: "The development factor from the last observed age to 'ultimate.' If your triangle goes to DY 10 but claims still trickle in after that, the tail factor (say 1.02) captures that remaining 2% of development. For short-tail lines (property), tail ≈ 1.00. For long-tail (asbestos, medical malpractice), the tail can be significant and is the most uncertain part of the analysis."

Q: "What is a calendar year effect?"

A: "Something that happens at a specific point in time affecting ALL accident years simultaneously. Example: company speeds up claims settlement in 2023 → all AYs show high incremental payments in 2023. This shows as a diagonal pattern in the triangle (all cells where AY + DY = 2023 are inflated). If not accounted for, it distorts forward-looking development factors."

Q: "How does reserving connect to pricing?"

A: "Pricing needs ULTIMATE loss estimates to set rates. If you price based on immature (undeveloped) claims, you'll underprice — because those claims will grow. Development factors from reserving tell pricing: 'multiply your 12-month claims by 2.0 to get the true cost.' Without this, the pricing model systematically underestimates recent years."

Q: "What's the difference between case reserves and IBNR?"

A: "Case reserves are set by claims adjusters on INDIVIDUAL known claims — their best estimate of what's still owed. IBNR is an AGGREGATE reserve set by actuaries covering: (1) pure IBNR — claims that happened but aren't reported yet, (2) development on known claims beyond what the adjuster estimated (IBNER — Incurred But Not Enough Reported). Total reserve = Case reserves + IBNR."

Q: "How do you select the final ultimate?"

A: "I compute Chain Ladder, BF, and Cape Cod. For mature years (>80% developed), I lean toward Chain Ladder because the data is reliable. For immature years (<60% developed), I lean toward BF/Cape Cod because CL is too sensitive to noise. I present all three to the actuarial team and we select based on judgment, often taking a weighted average. I document the rationale for each selection."
