Section 1: The Big Picture — What Is Capital Modelling?

Insurance companies collect premiums TODAY to pay claims TOMORROW. But what if a catastrophe happens and claims are WAY bigger than expected? The company needs a cash cushion — capital — to survive bad years.

Capital modelling = figuring out HOW MUCH cash cushion you need.
Solvency II = the European/Australian regulatory framework that tells insurers exactly how to calculate this.

Pricing → Capital connection:
• Pricing asks: "What's the EXPECTED cost?" (mean of loss distribution)
• Capital asks: "What's the WORST-CASE cost?" (tail of loss distribution)

You already model the distribution in pricing (Frequency × Severity). Capital modelling just asks: "What happens at the 99.5th percentile of that distribution?"

The Key Question Capital Modelling Answers

SCR (simplified) = VaR(99.5%) of losses over 1 year − Expected Loss

In other words: how much WORSE than average could it get in a really bad year?
If Expected Loss = $50M and the 99.5th percentile = $120M, then SCR ≈ $70M.

The Aggregate Loss Model — Where Pricing Meets Capital

Aggregate Loss S = X₁ + X₂ + ... + X_N

Where:
• N = number of claims (random) ~ Poisson(λ) or NegBin(r, p) ← FREQUENCY
• Xᵢ = size of claim i (random) ~ Lognormal(μ, σ) or Pareto(α, θ) ← SEVERITY
• S = total loss for the portfolio (what we need the distribution of)

In pricing: E[S] = E[N] × E[X] → the mean = your pure premium
In capital: VaR₉₉.₅%(S) → the 99.5th percentile = what drives SCR
You already model E[N] and E[X] in pricing (frequency × severity). For capital, you need the FULL DISTRIBUTION of S, not just the mean. You simulate it with Monte Carlo: draw random N, then draw N random claim sizes, sum them up. Do this 100,000 times. Sort the results. The 99.5th percentile is your VaR. 
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# AGGREGATE LOSS SIMULATION — The Foundation of Capital Modelling
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

np.random.seed(42)
n_simulations = 100_000

# ─── Parameters (from your PRICING model!) ──────────────
# These come directly from your GLM/GAM frequency-severity model
expected_frequency = 150      # Expected claims per year (λ)
mean_severity = 85_000        # Average claim size ($)
cv_severity = 2.5             # Coefficient of variation of severity

# Convert to lognormal parameters
sigma_sev = np.sqrt(np.log(1 + cv_severity**2))
mu_sev = np.log(mean_severity) - 0.5 * sigma_sev**2

# ─── Monte Carlo Simulation ─────────────────────────────
aggregate_losses = np.zeros(n_simulations)

for i in range(n_simulations):
    # Step 1: Draw number of claims (Frequency)
    n_claims = np.random.poisson(expected_frequency)
    
    # Step 2: Draw each claim's size (Severity)
    if n_claims > 0:
        claim_sizes = np.random.lognormal(mu_sev, sigma_sev, size=n_claims)
        aggregate_losses[i] = claim_sizes.sum()
    else:
        aggregate_losses[i] = 0

# ─── Results: From Pricing to Capital ───────────────────
mean_loss = aggregate_losses.mean()
var_995 = np.percentile(aggregate_losses, 99.5)
var_99 = np.percentile(aggregate_losses, 99.0)
es_995 = aggregate_losses[aggregate_losses >= var_995].mean()

print("="*60)
print("AGGREGATE LOSS DISTRIBUTION — PRICING TO CAPITAL")
print("="*60)
print(f"  Expected Loss (Mean):     ${mean_loss:>14,.0f}  ← PRICING uses this")
print(f"  Std Dev:                  ${aggregate_losses.std():>14,.0f}")
print(f"  VaR (99.0%):              ${var_99:>14,.0f}")
print(f"  VaR (99.5%):              ${var_995:>14,.0f}  ← SCR based on this")
print(f"  Expected Shortfall (99.5):${es_995:>14,.0f}  ← Tail severity")
print(f"  SCR ≈ VaR - Mean:         ${var_995 - mean_loss:>14,.0f}  ← CAPITAL needed")
print("="*60)
print(f"\n  The 1-in-200 year loss is {var_995/mean_loss:.1f}x the expected loss")
print(f"  You need ${(var_995-mean_loss)/1e6:.1f}M in capital above expected losses")
The bridge from pricing to capital in one sentence:
"Pricing gives you the MEAN of the aggregate loss distribution. Capital modelling gives you the TAIL."

You use the SAME frequency and severity models from pricing — you just simulate the full distribution instead of taking the expectation.

Section 3: The Solvency II Standard Formula

Not every company builds a full Monte Carlo model. The regulator provides a "standard formula" — a simplified calculation using factors and correlations. Think of it like a quick-and-dirty approximation. Companies can either:
• Use the standard formula (simpler, less accurate), or
• Build an internal model (complex, more accurate, requires regulatory approval)

SCR Structure — The Risk Modules

Premium Risk vs Reserve Risk — Where YOU Fit

📋 Premium Risk

Question: "How bad could NEXT YEAR's claims be, relative to what we expect?"

Source: Your pricing model's uncertainty. The premium you charge is based on E[Loss], but actual losses could be much higher.

Your input: Loss ratio volatility, frequency/severity distributions, cat exposure.

Standard formula: SCR_premium = Volume × σ × z(99.5%)

📋 Reserve Risk

Question: "How bad could our existing reserves be wrong?"

Source: Reserving uncertainty. We estimated IBNR = $50M but what if it's really $80M?

Your input: Reserve variability from triangle analysis, development factor uncertainty.

Standard formula: SCR_reserve = Outstanding reserves × σ × z(99.5%)

Standard Formula: Premium & Reserve Risk Calculation

Combined Premium & Reserve Risk SCR:

SCR_NL = 3 × σ × V

Where:
• V = Volume measure = max(earned premium, written premium) + outstanding reserves
• σ = Combined standard deviation (formula below)
• 3 = approximation for z(99.5%) assuming lognormal ≈ 3 standard deviations

σ combined:
σ² = [σ_prem² × V_prem² + σ_res² × V_res² + 2 × ρ × σ_prem × V_prem × σ_res × V_res] / V²

ρ = correlation between premium and reserve risk (standard formula: 0.5) 
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# SOLVENCY II STANDARD FORMULA — Premium & Reserve Risk
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

def standard_formula_nl_risk(
    earned_premium,      # Volume of premium risk
    outstanding_reserves, # Volume of reserve risk  
    sigma_premium,       # Volatility of premium risk (from table or company data)
    sigma_reserve,       # Volatility of reserve risk
    rho=0.5             # Correlation between premium and reserve risk
):
    """
    Solvency II Standard Formula: Non-Life Underwriting Risk (simplified).
    
    sigma_premium and sigma_reserve come from:
    - Standard formula: prescribed by regulator per line of business
    - USP (Undertaking-Specific Parameters): computed from YOUR data
    - Internal model: full simulation
    """
    
    V_prem = earned_premium
    V_res = outstanding_reserves
    V_total = V_prem + V_res
    
    # Combined sigma (variance formula with correlation)
    variance = (
        (sigma_premium * V_prem)**2 + 
        (sigma_reserve * V_res)**2 + 
        2 * rho * sigma_premium * V_prem * sigma_reserve * V_res
    )
    sigma_combined = np.sqrt(variance) / V_total
    
    # SCR = 3 * sigma * V (the "3" approximates 99.5th percentile of lognormal)
    SCR = 3 * sigma_combined * V_total
    
    print(f"{'='*55}")
    print(f"STANDARD FORMULA — Non-Life Underwriting Risk")
    print(f"{'='*55}")
    print(f"  Earned Premium:        ${earned_premium:>12,.0f}")
    print(f"  Outstanding Reserves:  ${outstanding_reserves:>12,.0f}")
    print(f"  Total Volume:          ${V_total:>12,.0f}")
    print(f"  σ (premium):           {sigma_premium:.1%}")
    print(f"  σ (reserve):           {sigma_reserve:.1%}")
    print(f"  ρ (correlation):       {rho:.2f}")
    print(f"  σ (combined):          {sigma_combined:.3%}")
    print(f"{'─'*55}")
    print(f"  SCR (Non-Life):        ${SCR:>12,.0f}")
    print(f"{'='*55}")
    
    return SCR

# ─── Example: General Liability (Construction) ──────────
# Standard formula prescribed sigmas for GL:
#   Premium risk σ: ~10% (varies by country/regulator)
#   Reserve risk σ: ~11%

scr = standard_formula_nl_risk(
    earned_premium=80_000_000,       # $80M earned premium
    outstanding_reserves=120_000_000, # $120M reserves
    sigma_premium=0.10,              # 10% volatility
    sigma_reserve=0.11,              # 11% volatility
    rho=0.5                          # Prescribed correlation
)
QBE context: As a GL pricing analyst, your frequency/severity models directly feed the Premium Risk calculation. If you can quantify the uncertainty in your pricing estimates (e.g., "my model predicts $60M loss ratio ± 15%"), that ±15% IS the sigma that drives capital. Better pricing models → more accurate capital → company doesn't hold too much (expensive) or too little (risky).

# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# INTERNAL CAPITAL MODEL — Full Multi-Line Simulation
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from scipy.stats import lognorm, poisson, norm

np.random.seed(42)
N_SIM = 100_000

# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# STEP 1: Define each Line of Business (from your pricing models)
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

lines_of_business = {
    'GL_Construction': {
        'expected_frequency': 200,        # Expected claims per year
        'freq_distribution': 'poisson',   
        'mean_severity': 95_000,          # Average claim size
        'severity_cv': 2.8,               # High CV = heavy tail (construction!)
        'severity_distribution': 'lognormal',
        'earned_premium': 30_000_000,     # $30M premium
    },
    'Property': {
        'expected_frequency': 500,
        'freq_distribution': 'poisson',
        'mean_severity': 45_000,
        'severity_cv': 1.8,
        'severity_distribution': 'lognormal',
        'earned_premium': 50_000_000,
    },
    'Motor': {
        'expected_frequency': 2000,
        'freq_distribution': 'poisson',
        'mean_severity': 12_000,
        'severity_cv': 1.5,
        'severity_distribution': 'lognormal',
        'earned_premium': 40_000_000,
    },
}

# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# STEP 2: Simulate each line independently first
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

def simulate_aggregate_loss(params, n_sim):
    """Simulate aggregate loss for one line of business."""
    lam = params['expected_frequency']
    mu_x = params['mean_severity']
    cv = params['severity_cv']
    
    # Lognormal parameters from mean and CV
    sigma = np.sqrt(np.log(1 + cv**2))
    mu = np.log(mu_x) - 0.5 * sigma**2
    
    losses = np.zeros(n_sim)
    for i in range(n_sim):
        n_claims = np.random.poisson(lam)
        if n_claims > 0:
            claims = np.random.lognormal(mu, sigma, size=n_claims)
            losses[i] = claims.sum()
    
    return losses

# Simulate each line
simulated_losses = {}
for line, params in lines_of_business.items():
    simulated_losses[line] = simulate_aggregate_loss(params, N_SIM)
    print(f"{line}: Mean=${simulated_losses[line].mean()/1e6:.1f}M, "
          f"VaR99.5=${np.percentile(simulated_losses[line], 99.5)/1e6:.1f}M")

# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# STEP 3: Introduce DEPENDENCIES using Copulas
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# Lines of business are NOT independent!
# Bad economy → more GL claims AND more property claims

# Correlation matrix between lines
correlation_matrix = np.array([
    # GL    Prop   Motor
    [1.00,  0.30,  0.15],  # GL
    [0.30,  1.00,  0.10],  # Property
    [0.15,  0.10,  1.00],  # Motor
])

# Generate correlated uniform random variables using Gaussian Copula
# (This is the FRM copula concept in action!)
from scipy.stats import norm as norm_dist

def gaussian_copula_samples(corr_matrix, n_sim):
    """Generate correlated uniform samples via Gaussian copula."""
    n_vars = corr_matrix.shape[0]
    
    # Step 1: Cholesky decomposition of correlation matrix
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(corr_matrix)
    
    # Step 2: Generate independent standard normals
    Z = np.random.standard_normal((n_sim, n_vars))
    
    # Step 3: Correlate them
    correlated_normals = Z @ L.T
    
    # Step 4: Transform to uniform [0,1] using normal CDF
    uniforms = norm_dist.cdf(correlated_normals)
    
    return uniforms

# Generate correlated samples
U = gaussian_copula_samples(correlation_matrix, N_SIM)

# Now use these correlated uniforms to "re-order" our simulated losses
# This preserves marginal distributions but introduces dependency
def apply_copula_dependency(simulated_losses_dict, uniforms):
    """
    Re-order simulated losses to match copula-implied dependency.
    Uses the rank-correlation approach.
    """
    lines = list(simulated_losses_dict.keys())
    dependent_losses = {}
    
    for i, line in enumerate(lines):
        # Sort the simulated losses
        sorted_losses = np.sort(simulated_losses_dict[line])
        # Use copula uniforms to pick quantiles
        # Convert uniform to index
        indices = (uniforms[:, i] * (N_SIM - 1)).astype(int)
        dependent_losses[line] = sorted_losses[indices]
    
    return dependent_losses

dependent_losses = apply_copula_dependency(simulated_losses, U)

# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# STEP 4: Aggregate across all lines
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

total_loss = sum(dependent_losses.values())

# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# STEP 5: Compute Capital Metrics
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

total_premium = sum(p['earned_premium'] for p in lines_of_business.values())
expected_loss = total_loss.mean()
var_995 = np.percentile(total_loss, 99.5)
var_99 = np.percentile(total_loss, 99.0)
es_995 = total_loss[total_loss >= var_995].mean()

# SCR = VaR - Expected (capital above what we've priced for)
scr = var_995 - expected_loss

# Diversification benefit
sum_individual_scrs = sum(
    np.percentile(dependent_losses[line], 99.5) - dependent_losses[line].mean()
    for line in dependent_losses
)
diversification_benefit = sum_individual_scrs - scr

print(f"\n{'='*60}")
print(f"INTERNAL MODEL RESULTS")
print(f"{'='*60}")
print(f"  Total Premium:          ${total_premium/1e6:>8.1f}M")
print(f"  Expected Total Loss:    ${expected_loss/1e6:>8.1f}M")
print(f"  Loss Ratio (expected):  {expected_loss/total_premium:.1%}")
print(f"{'─'*60}")
print(f"  VaR (99.0%):            ${var_99/1e6:>8.1f}M")
print(f"  VaR (99.5%):            ${var_995/1e6:>8.1f}M  ← 1-in-200 loss")
print(f"  Expected Shortfall:     ${es_995/1e6:>8.1f}M")
print(f"{'─'*60}")
print(f"  SCR (VaR - Mean):       ${scr/1e6:>8.1f}M  ← CAPITAL REQUIRED")
print(f"  Sum of individual SCRs: ${sum_individual_scrs/1e6:>8.1f}M")
print(f"  Diversification Benefit:${diversification_benefit/1e6:>8.1f}M ({diversification_benefit/sum_individual_scrs:.0%} reduction)")
print(f"{'='*60}")
Diversification Benefit: Because GL, Property, and Motor aren't perfectly correlated (ρ < 1), the combined SCR is LESS than the sum of individual SCRs. This is the same concept as portfolio diversification in FRM — imperfect correlations reduce total risk. The diversification benefit is often 20-35% in practice.

Section 5: Parameter Uncertainty — Making It Realistic

In the simulation above, we used FIXED parameters (λ=200, μ_severity=95000). But in reality, we ESTIMATED those parameters from data — they have uncertainty! A proper capital model accounts for this: "not only is the loss random given our parameters, but the parameters themselves are uncertain."
FRM Connection: This is the "model risk" concept from FRM. Your VaR estimate has estimation error. In insurance, we handle this by putting distributions on the parameters themselves (Bayesian approach) or by bootstrapping. 
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# PARAMETER UNCERTAINTY — Two-Stage Simulation
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

def simulate_with_parameter_uncertainty(
    expected_freq,    # Central estimate of frequency
    freq_cv,         # Uncertainty in frequency estimate (e.g., 15%)
    mean_sev,        # Central estimate of severity  
    sev_cv,          # CV of individual claim severity
    sev_param_cv,    # Uncertainty in the severity PARAMETER (e.g., 10%)
    n_sim=100_000
):
    """
    Two-stage simulation:
    Stage 1: Draw parameters from their uncertainty distributions
    Stage 2: Given those parameters, simulate claims
    
    This captures BOTH process risk (randomness of claims) 
    AND parameter risk (uncertainty in our estimates).
    """
    losses = np.zeros(n_sim)
    
    for i in range(n_sim):
        # ─── Stage 1: Parameter Uncertainty ─────────────
        # True frequency could be higher or lower than our estimate
        # Model freq parameter as Gamma (positive, skewed)
        freq_shape = 1 / freq_cv**2
        freq_scale = expected_freq * freq_cv**2
        true_freq = np.random.gamma(freq_shape, freq_scale)
        
        # True mean severity has uncertainty too
        sev_shape = 1 / sev_param_cv**2
        sev_scale = mean_sev * sev_param_cv**2
        true_mean_sev = np.random.gamma(sev_shape, sev_scale)
        
        # ─── Stage 2: Process Risk (given true parameters) ─
        n_claims = np.random.poisson(true_freq)
        
        if n_claims > 0:
            sigma = np.sqrt(np.log(1 + sev_cv**2))
            mu = np.log(true_mean_sev) - 0.5 * sigma**2
            claims = np.random.lognormal(mu, sigma, size=n_claims)
            losses[i] = claims.sum()
    
    return losses

# ─── Compare: With and Without Parameter Uncertainty ─────
losses_no_param_unc = simulate_aggregate_loss(
    lines_of_business['GL_Construction'], N_SIM
)

losses_with_param_unc = simulate_with_parameter_uncertainty(
    expected_freq=200,
    freq_cv=0.15,        # 15% uncertainty in freq estimate
    mean_sev=95_000,
    sev_cv=2.8,
    sev_param_cv=0.12,   # 12% uncertainty in sev estimate
    n_sim=N_SIM
)

print("WITHOUT parameter uncertainty:")
print(f"  VaR(99.5%) = ${np.percentile(losses_no_param_unc, 99.5)/1e6:.1f}M")
print(f"\nWITH parameter uncertainty:")
print(f"  VaR(99.5%) = ${np.percentile(losses_with_param_unc, 99.5)/1e6:.1f}M")
print(f"\n  → Parameter uncertainty adds ~{((np.percentile(losses_with_param_unc,99.5)/np.percentile(losses_no_param_unc,99.5))-1)*100:.0f}% to the capital requirement")
Key insight for the interview: Parameter uncertainty typically adds 10-30% to the capital requirement. This is why having BETTER data and BETTER models (like your GLM/GAM) directly reduces capital. Better pricing accuracy → lower parameter uncertainty → lower SCR → company can write more business with the same capital.

Section 6: Copulas & Dependency — The FRM Deep Dive

FRM Part 1 covered: Gaussian copula, t-copula, tail dependence, Spearman's rho vs Kendall's tau. All of this applies directly to capital modelling. The 2008 financial crisis showed that Gaussian copulas underestimate tail dependence — the same lesson applies to insurance.

Why Dependency Matters for Capital

If GL and Property are independent: the chance BOTH have a bad year simultaneously = 0.5% × 0.5% = 0.0025% (basically impossible).

If they're correlated (e.g., economic recession causes more claims in both): the chance both have a bad year is much higher. More co-movement in the tail = MORE capital needed.

Copula Choices and Their Properties

Student-tSymmetric tail dependenceWhen extremes co-occurEconomic recession hits all lines
ClaytonLower tail onlyWhen bad events are correlated but good events aren'tCatastrophes affecting multiple lines

# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# COPULA COMPARISON — Gaussian vs t-Copula
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
from scipy.stats import t as t_dist

def t_copula_samples(corr_matrix, df, n_sim):
    """
    Generate samples from a t-copula.
    Key difference from Gaussian: HEAVIER TAILS → more tail dependence.
    
    df = degrees of freedom:
      - df → ∞: converges to Gaussian copula
      - df = 3-5: significant tail dependence
    """
    n_vars = corr_matrix.shape[0]
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(corr_matrix)
    
    # Generate from multivariate t-distribution
    Z = np.random.standard_normal((n_sim, n_vars))
    correlated_normals = Z @ L.T
    
    # Mix with chi-squared to get t-distribution
    chi2 = np.random.chisquare(df, size=n_sim)
    scaling = np.sqrt(df / chi2)
    correlated_t = correlated_normals * scaling[:, np.newaxis]
    
    # Transform to uniform using t-CDF
    uniforms = t_dist.cdf(correlated_t, df)
    
    return uniforms

# Compare Gaussian vs t-copula for SCR
# Gaussian: no tail dependence
U_gaussian = gaussian_copula_samples(correlation_matrix, N_SIM)
dep_losses_gaussian = apply_copula_dependency(simulated_losses, U_gaussian)
total_gaussian = sum(dep_losses_gaussian.values())

# t-copula (df=4): significant tail dependence  
U_t = t_copula_samples(correlation_matrix, df=4, n_sim=N_SIM)
dep_losses_t = apply_copula_dependency(simulated_losses, U_t)
total_t = sum(dep_losses_t.values())

print(f"Gaussian Copula VaR(99.5%): ${np.percentile(total_gaussian, 99.5)/1e6:.1f}M")
print(f"t-Copula (df=4) VaR(99.5%): ${np.percentile(total_t, 99.5)/1e6:.1f}M")
print(f"  → t-copula gives {((np.percentile(total_t,99.5)/np.percentile(total_gaussian,99.5))-1)*100:.1f}% higher capital")
print(f"  → Because extreme losses across lines are MORE likely to co-occur")
Interview gold: "The choice of copula significantly impacts the SCR. A Gaussian copula assumes no tail dependence — extreme events in different lines are independent. A t-copula introduces tail dependence: when one line has a catastrophic year, others are more likely to as well. In practice, the t-copula often adds 5-15% to the diversified SCR compared to Gaussian, and is more realistic for insurance portfolios where systemic events (economic downturns, pandemics) affect multiple lines simultaneously."

Section 7: Reserve Risk in the Capital Model

Remember your loss triangle work? Reserving gives you IBNR estimates. But those estimates are uncertain — the triangle could develop differently than expected. Reserve risk captures: "How wrong could our reserves be?"

Connecting Your Triangle Work to Capital

# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# RESERVE RISK: Bootstrap the Triangle
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# The Mack method gives a closed-form estimate of reserve variability
# The Bootstrap method gives a full distribution via simulation

def bootstrap_chain_ladder(cum_triangle, n_bootstrap=10_000):
    """
    Bootstrap the Chain Ladder to get a DISTRIBUTION of ultimate losses.
    This distribution's tail gives you the reserve risk capital.
    
    Method: Resample the age-to-age factors with replacement,
    re-project the triangle, get a different ultimate each time.
    """
    dev_years = sorted(cum_triangle.columns)
    n_dy = len(dev_years)
    
    # Compute individual ATA factors for each AY
    all_factors = {}  # {dy_period: [list of individual AY factors]}
    for i in range(n_dy - 1):
        col_now = dev_years[i]
        col_next = dev_years[i + 1]
        mask = cum_triangle[col_now].notna() & cum_triangle[col_next].notna()
        factors = (cum_triangle.loc[mask, col_next] / cum_triangle.loc[mask, col_now]).values
        all_factors[i] = factors
    
    # Bootstrap: resample factors and re-project
    bootstrap_ultimates = np.zeros(n_bootstrap)
    
    for b in range(n_bootstrap):
        # Resample: for each development period, randomly pick factors
        resampled_cdfs = {}
        for dy_idx in range(n_dy):
            if dy_idx < n_dy - 1:
                # Pick a random factor from the available ones
                sampled_factor = np.random.choice(all_factors[dy_idx])
                resampled_cdfs[dev_years[dy_idx]] = sampled_factor
            else:
                resampled_cdfs[dev_years[dy_idx]] = 1.0
        
        # Compute CDF to ultimate for each starting DY
        total_ultimate = 0
        for ay in cum_triangle.index:
            row = cum_triangle.loc[ay].dropna()
            latest_dy_idx = list(cum_triangle.columns).index(row.index[-1])
            latest_value = row.iloc[-1]
            
            # Multiply remaining factors
            cdf = 1.0
            for k in range(latest_dy_idx, n_dy - 1):
                cdf *= resampled_cdfs[dev_years[k]]
            
            total_ultimate += latest_value * cdf
        
        bootstrap_ultimates[b] = total_ultimate
    
    return bootstrap_ultimates

Section 8: Catastrophe Risk & Extreme Value Theory

FRM Connection: You studied EVT — GPD (Generalized Pareto Distribution) for modelling extreme tails, threshold selection, Hill estimator. In insurance, EVT models the very largest claims (mega-catastrophes) that drive the 99.5th percentile.

Why Normal Distributions Fail for Insurance Capital

The problem: Insurance losses are HEAVY-TAILED. A single catastrophe can cost more than 10 years of normal losses combined. If you assume Normal distribution, you'll massively UNDERESTIMATE the 99.5th percentile.

Actual insurance loss distributions have:
• Right skewness: Many small claims, few huge ones
• Excess kurtosis: More extreme events than Normal predicts
• Fat tails: Probability of 5× expected is much higher than Normal implies 
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# EVT + INSURANCE: Modelling Large Claims Separately
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
from scipy.stats import genpareto

# ─── Approach: Separate "attritional" from "large" claims ───
# Attritional: Normal claims < threshold (e.g., < $500K)
# Large: Claims > threshold → model with GPD (from FRM EVT!)

def simulate_with_large_claims(
    attritional_freq, attritional_mean_sev, attritional_cv,
    large_freq,      # Expected number of large claims per year
    threshold,       # GPD threshold (e.g., $500K)
    gpd_shape,       # ξ (xi) parameter: >0 means heavy tail (Pareto-like)
    gpd_scale,       # σ parameter
    n_sim=100_000
):
    """
    Split model: attritional claims + large claims (EVT/GPD).
    This is how sophisticated internal models work.
    """
    losses = np.zeros(n_sim)
    
    for i in range(n_sim):
        # Attritional claims (many small claims)
        n_att = np.random.poisson(attritional_freq)
        sigma = np.sqrt(np.log(1 + attritional_cv**2))
        mu = np.log(attritional_mean_sev) - 0.5 * sigma**2
        if n_att > 0:
            att_claims = np.random.lognormal(mu, sigma, size=n_att)
            att_claims = att_claims[att_claims < threshold]  # Cap at threshold
            att_total = att_claims.sum()
        else:
            att_total = 0
        
        # Large claims (few but huge) — modelled with GPD
        n_large = np.random.poisson(large_freq)
        if n_large > 0:
            # GPD excess above threshold
            excesses = genpareto.rvs(gpd_shape, scale=gpd_scale, size=n_large)
            large_claims = threshold + excesses
            large_total = large_claims.sum()
        else:
            large_total = 0
        
        losses[i] = att_total + large_total
    
    return losses

# Example: GL Construction with large claims
losses_split = simulate_with_large_claims(
    attritional_freq=180,
    attritional_mean_sev=60_000,
    attritional_cv=1.5,
    large_freq=5,              # ~5 large claims per year
    threshold=500_000,         # Large = > $500K
    gpd_shape=0.7,             # ξ=0.7 → heavy Pareto tail (infinite variance!)

print(f"  → This is much higher than a Normal would predict")
GPD shape parameter ξ (xi) interpretation:
• ξ = 0: Exponential tail (light) — rare for insurance
• ξ = 0.3-0.5: Moderate heavy tail — typical for GL claims
• ξ = 0.7-1.0: Very heavy tail — construction defects, asbestos
• ξ > 1: Infinite mean! (extreme — catastrophe losses can approach this)

For GL Construction at QBE, ξ is probably 0.4-0.8. This means the ratio of VaR(99.5%)/Mean is likely 3x-6x, not the 1.5x that Normal would predict.

Section 9: Capital Allocation — How Capital Gets Assigned to Lines

Total company SCR = $200M. But there are 5 lines of business. How much capital does each line "use"? This matters because:
• Lines that use more capital should earn higher returns (to justify the capital)
• Capital allocation directly impacts pricing — the capital cost is loaded into the premium
• Performance is measured as Return on Allocated Capital (ROAC)

Capital Allocation Methods

MethodLogicFormula
Proportional to standalone SCRSimple. Each line gets capital in proportion to its standalone risk.Alloc_i = SCR_i / Σ(SCR_j) × Total SCR
Euler / MarginalEach line gets capital equal to its MARGINAL contribution to total risk. Mathematically elegant, adds up to total.Alloc_i = E[L_i | L_total = VaR(99.5%)]
Shapley ValueGame theory approach. Average marginal contribution across all possible orderings.Complex — rarely used in practice
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# CAPITAL ALLOCATION — Euler Method (Industry Standard)
# ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

def euler_allocation(dependent_losses_dict, total_losses, confidence=99.5):
    """
    Euler (marginal) capital allocation.
    
    For each line, allocate capital = E[L_i | Total ≈ VaR(99.5%)]
    i.e., "When total losses are at the 99.5th percentile, 
           what was this line's contribution on average?"
    
    This is THE most theoretically sound allocation method.
    """
    var_level = np.percentile(total_losses, confidence)
    
    # Find simulations where total loss is "near" VaR
    # Use a band: 99.0% to 99.9%
    lower = np.percentile(total_losses, confidence - 0.5)
    upper = np.percentile(total_losses, confidence + 0.4)
    mask = (total_losses >= lower) & (total_losses <= upper)
    
    allocations = {}
    for line, losses in dependent_losses_dict.items():
        # Average contribution of this line when total is near VaR
        allocations[line] = losses[mask].mean()
    
    # Normalize to sum to total SCR
    total_alloc = sum(allocations.values())
    scr_total = var_level - total_losses.mean()
    
    print(f"\n{'='*60}")
    print(f"EULER CAPITAL ALLOCATION")
    print(f"{'='*60}")
    print(f"  Total SCR: ${scr_total/1e6:.1f}M")
    print(f"{'─'*60}")
    
    for line in allocations:
        alloc_scr = (allocations[line] / total_alloc) * scr_total
        standalone_scr = np.percentile(dependent_losses_dict[line], 99.5) - dependent_losses_dict[line].mean()
        div_benefit = standalone_scr - alloc_scr
        print(f"  {line:20s}: Allocated=${alloc_scr/1e6:.1f}M "
              f"(Standalone=${standalone_scr/1e6:.1f}M, "
              f"Div Benefit=${div_benefit/1e6:.1f}M)")
    
    print(f"{'='*60}")
    return allocations

Interview Q&A

Q: "What is the SCR?"

A: "The Solvency Capital Requirement is the amount of capital an insurer must hold to survive a 1-in-200 year adverse event. Technically, it's the 99.5% VaR of the change in basic own funds over a one-year time horizon. It ensures the company can meet its obligations to policyholders with 99.5% probability."

Q: "How does pricing connect to capital?"

A: "Pricing provides the expected loss (mean). Capital looks at how bad losses could be (tail). Both use the same underlying frequency-severity model — capital just extends it to the full distribution. Additionally, capital costs are loaded back into the premium, so there's a direct feedback loop: riskier business needs more capital, which costs more, which must be reflected in higher premiums."

Q: "Explain diversification benefit."

A: "Because different lines of business aren't perfectly correlated, the combined capital requirement is less than the sum of individual capitals. For example, if GL needs $30M and Property needs $40M standalone, together they might only need $55M (not $70M) because it's unlikely both have their worst year simultaneously. The $15M difference is the diversification benefit. We model this using copulas to capture the dependency structure."

Q: "Why not just use the Normal distribution?"

A: "Insurance losses are heavily right-skewed and fat-tailed. A Normal distribution would massively underestimate the 99.5th percentile. We use lognormal or Pareto distributions for severity, and for the extreme tail, Generalized Pareto (from EVT). The ratio of VaR(99.5%)/Mean for insurance is typically 2-5x, while a Normal would suggest ~1.5x. Using Normal = holding too little capital."

Q: "Standard formula vs internal model?"

A: "The standard formula uses prescribed factors and correlations — one size fits all. An internal model uses company-specific data, distributions, and copulas. Internal models are more accurate (often resulting in lower SCR through better-captured diversification) but require significant investment and regulatory approval. The standard formula is a floor — companies can use it immediately without approval."

Q: "What is parameter uncertainty?"

A: "Our model parameters (frequency λ, severity μ) are estimates from data — they have confidence intervals. Parameter uncertainty means: even if the world stays the same, our estimates could be wrong. A proper capital model simulates this: first draw the 'true' parameters from their uncertainty distribution, THEN simulate claims. This typically adds 10-30% to the capital requirement. Better data and better models reduce parameter uncertainty."

Q: "What copula would you use?"

A: "I'd start with a Gaussian copula as a baseline — it's simple and widely used. Then I'd compare with a t-copula (df=4-8) which introduces tail dependence: the property that when one line has extreme losses, other lines are more likely to as well. For insurance, t-copula is more realistic because systemic events (economic downturns, regulatory changes) affect multiple lines. The t-copula typically adds 5-15% to the diversified SCR."

Q: "How many simulations do you need?"

A: "For VaR at 99.5%, you need the estimate to be stable at the 99.5th percentile. With 100,000 simulations, you have 500 observations above the 99.5th percentile — enough for a stable estimate. I'd validate by running the model multiple times with different seeds and checking that the SCR doesn't move by more than ±2-3%. For regulatory submissions, companies often run 250,000-1,000,000."

Q: "VaR vs Expected Shortfall — which is better?"

A: "VaR tells you the THRESHOLD of a bad year (you won't lose more than $X with 99.5% probability). But it says nothing about HOW BAD it gets beyond that point. Expected Shortfall (CVaR) = average loss GIVEN you're beyond VaR. ES is sub-additive (diversification always helps), unlike VaR. Solvency II uses VaR(99.5%), but internal risk management often uses ES because it better captures tail severity. Swiss Solvency Test uses ES."

Q: "How does reinsurance affect the capital model?"

A: "Reinsurance truncates the loss distribution — an excess-of-loss treaty with $2M retention means no individual claim costs us more than $2M. This massively reduces the tail (VaR). In the capital model, we simulate losses GROSS, then apply reinsurance recoveries to get NET losses, and compute SCR on the net. The capital saving from reinsurance must be weighed against the reinsurance premium cost."
