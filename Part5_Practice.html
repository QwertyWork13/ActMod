Chapter 34: Large Losses — Capping, Truncation & ILFs

One huge $50M claim can distort your entire model. It's like one student who scored 1000% on a test — the average suddenly looks wrong for everyone. We "cap" large losses to prevent this, model the capped data, and then add a separate "large loss load" on top.

Why Cap Large Losses?

Without Capping

  • One $50M claim dominates the model
  • That policy's country/trade gets unfairly high relativity
  • Model is unstable year-to-year
  • Predictions are driven by randomness

With Capping at $5M

  • $50M claim becomes $5M (cap applied)
  • Model learns from attritional losses
  • Stable, reliable relativities
  • Large loss load added separately

How to Choose the Cap

Common approaches:
1. Percentile-based: Cap at 95th or 99th percentile of claim sizes
2. Fixed threshold: Cap at a round number (e.g., $1M, $5M) based on the book
3. X times average: Cap at 10× or 20× the average claim size
4. Business-driven: Cap at the retention level (what you keep vs reinsure) 
# R: Apply cap
cap <- quantile(df$claims[df$claims > 0], 0.99)  # 99th percentile
df$claims_capped <- pmin(df$claims, cap)

# Or fixed cap:
df$claims_capped <- pmin(df$claims, 5000000)  # Cap at $5M

Increased Limit Factors (ILFs)

A policy with a $10M limit should cost more than one with a $1M limit. ILFs tell you exactly how much more. If ILF($10M) = 2.5, then a $10M policy should cost 2.5× a $1M policy (all else equal).
ILF(L) = E[Claims capped at L] ÷ E[Claims capped at basic limit]

Example with basic limit = $1M:
ILF($1M) = 1.00 (by definition)
ILF($2M) = 1.45 (45% more for twice the limit)
ILF($5M) = 2.10
ILF($10M) = 2.50
ILFs are typically derived from severity distributions (Pareto, lognormal, or mixed exponential) fitted to historical large losses. They represent the "cost of higher limits" and are a core component of commercial liability pricing.

Chapter 35: IBNR & Development Factors

When a construction worker gets injured today, the claim might not be reported for 6 months, and the final cost might not be known for 5 years (lawsuits, medical treatment, etc.). IBNR = "Incurred But Not Reported" — the money we know we'll owe but haven't seen yet. Development factors estimate how much bigger claims will grow.

The Problem: Claims Develop Over Time

Development Factors (Link Ratios)

Development Factor = Claims at age (n+1) ÷ Claims at age n

Example (GL Construction):
• 12→24 month factor: 1.65 (claims grow 65%)
• 24→36 month factor: 1.15 (grow another 15%)
• 36→48 month factor: 1.05 (nearly stable)
• 48→ultimate factor: 1.02 (final tail)

Cumulative factor at 12 months = 1.65 × 1.15 × 1.05 × 1.02 = 2.01
→ Claims at 12 months will roughly DOUBLE by ultimate.

Why This Matters for Pricing

Your model uses developed claims (ultimate estimates), not raw incurred claims. If you trained on raw claims from recent years (2021-2022), those claims are immature — they'll grow 50-100%. The development factors adjust them to ultimate before modelling. Without this adjustment, your model would systematically underprice recent years.

Chapter 36: Credibility & Shrinkage

Imagine a country that had only 5 policies in your data. One of them had a huge claim. Should the country relativity be 5.0× (based on that tiny sample)? Of course not — the data is unreliable. Credibility blends unreliable small-sample estimates with a more stable "average" — the less data you have, the more you lean on the average.

The Credibility Formula

Credibility-weighted estimate = Z × (observed rate) + (1 − Z) × (prior rate)

Z = credibility factor (0 to 1)
• Z = 1 → fully trust the data (lots of claims)
• Z = 0 → ignore the data, use the average (no claims)

Common formula: Z = n / (n + K) where n = number of claims, K = credibility constant
If K = 50 and n = 100 claims: Z = 100/150 = 0.67 → 67% data, 33% average

How Credibility Appears in Your Model

The GAM naturally provides credibility-like behavior through the smoothing penalty. Categories with sparse data (e.g., "Asia" with few claims) have their coefficients pulled toward zero (the average). Categories with lots of data (e.g., "Australia") get coefficients closer to the raw data. This is called shrinkage — it's credibility done automatically by the model.
In interviews: If asked "how do you handle categories with small volume?", mention three things:
1. Grouping (merge sparse categories together)
2. Credibility weighting (Z formula)
3. Regularization (Ridge/Lasso penalty shrinks sparse coefficients toward zero)

Chapter 37: Model Deployment & Monitoring

Building the model is only half the job. Deploying it means putting it into the hands of underwriters (via rating tables or a quoting system). Monitoring means checking every quarter: "Is the model still accurate, or has the world changed?"

The Deployment Pipeline

  1. Build the model (what you've learned in this course)
  2. Extract relativities → Create rating tables in Excel
  3. Peer review → Another actuary reviews code, data, and assumptions
  4. Management sign-off → Chief Actuary approves
  5. Implement in quoting system → IT loads the tables into the pricing tool
  6. Parallel run → Run old AND new model side-by-side for 1-3 months
  7. Go live → New model becomes the official pricing basis
  8. Monitor quarterly → Track A/E ratios, Gini, portfolio mix changes

Model drift: A model built on 2018-2022 data may not work well in 2025 if:
• Inflation changes claim sizes (social inflation in the US)
• New construction methods change risk profiles
• Regulatory changes affect liability
• Portfolio shifts to different countries/trades

Re-fit the model at least annually with fresh data.

Chapter 40: Interview Questions — Easy, Medium & Hard

These 25 questions cover the most commonly asked topics in insurance pricing interviews. For each question, try to answer it yourself before clicking the answer.

EASY (5 Questions)

EASY

Q1: What distribution do you use for insurance claims, and why?

Click to reveal answer

Tweedie distribution (compound Poisson-Gamma) with a log link. Insurance claims have two key features: (1) many policies have zero claims, and (2) non-zero claims are positive and right-skewed. Tweedie naturally handles this mix of zeros and positive continuous values. The power parameter p (typically 1.3–1.7) controls the zero-mass. We use p ≈ 1.5 for construction GL. Normal distribution fails because it predicts negative claims. Poisson is for counts only. Gamma can't handle zeros.

EASY

Q2: What is a link function? Why do we use the log link?

Click to reveal answer

A link function g() transforms the expected value μ to the linear predictor scale: g(μ) = η = β₀ + β₁X₁ + ... The log link means log(μ) = η, so μ = eη. Benefits: (1) predictions are always positive (can't predict negative premiums), (2) effects are multiplicative — each factor multiplies the premium, which is how rating tables work, (3) it's the canonical link for Poisson and works well with Gamma/Tweedie.

EASY

Q3: What is a relativity? How do you interpret β = 0.26 for UK?

Click to reveal answer

A relativity is eβ, the multiplicative factor for that category relative to the base. β_UK = 0.26 → relativity = e0.26 = 1.30. This means UK policies are 30% more expensive than the base (Australia). In the rating table, the underwriter multiplies the base premium by 1.30 for UK risks.

EASY

Q4: What is an offset in a GLM? Give an example.

Click to reveal answer

An offset is a variable with a fixed coefficient of 1 (not estimated by the model). We use offset = log(PolicyDuration) to make the model proportional to duration: a 2-year policy should have exactly 2× the expected claims. This means the model estimates the annual RATE, and the offset scales it by duration. Without the offset, the model would waste a parameter estimating what we already know.

EASY

Q5: What is the difference between a GLM and a GAM?

Click to reveal answer

A GLM models continuous predictors as straight lines (on the link scale): log(μ) = β₀ + β₁X. A GAM replaces the linear term with a smooth function: log(μ) = β₀ + f(X). The smooth f(X) is built from splines (piecewise polynomials), allowing the model to capture non-linear relationships. Categorical variables are handled the same way in both. GAM = GLM + smooth terms for continuous variables.

MEDIUM (10 Questions)

MEDIUM

Q6: Explain the Frequency × Severity approach vs the Tweedie approach.

Click to reveal answer

Freq × Sev: Build two separate models — Poisson for frequency (how many claims) and Gamma for severity (how large). Multiply predictions: E[Claims] = E[Freq] × E[Sev]. Advantage: separate insights into what drives frequency vs severity. Tweedie: One model for the combined loss cost directly. The Tweedie distribution is mathematically equivalent to a compound Poisson-Gamma, so it implicitly does freq × sev. Advantage: simpler, one model to maintain. In practice, Tweedie gives very similar predictions to explicit freq × sev.

MEDIUM

Q7: How do you handle large losses in a pricing model?

Click to reveal answer

Cap (truncate) claims at a threshold (e.g., 99th percentile or a fixed amount like $5M). Model the capped claims to get stable attritional relativities. Then add a large loss load on top — estimated separately from severity distributions (Pareto/lognormal) fitted to large claims above the cap. This separates the attritional pricing (model-driven) from the large loss pricing (actuarial judgment). For different limits, use ILFs (Increased Limit Factors).

MEDIUM

Q8: What is cross-validation and why is it important?

Click to reveal answer

K-fold CV splits data into K equal parts. For each fold, train on K-1 parts and test on the held-out part. Average the test metrics across all folds. It's important because: (1) it estimates out-of-sample performance (will the model work on new data?), (2) it detects overfitting (model that fits training data but fails on test data), (3) it enables honest comparison between candidate models. I use 20-fold CV with Gini, AIC, deviance, and RMSE as metrics.

MEDIUM

Q9: What is the Gini coefficient in pricing, and what's a good value?

Click to reveal answer

The Gini coefficient measures how well the model ranks risks from low to high. It's calculated from the Lorenz curve: sort policies by predicted risk, plot cumulative actual claims vs cumulative policies. Gini = 2 × area between the model curve and the 45° diagonal. Range: 0 (random) to 1 (perfect). For insurance pricing, Gini = 0.20–0.30 is decent, 0.30–0.40 is good, > 0.40 is very good. My construction model achieves Gini ≈ 0.37.

MEDIUM

Q10: How do you decide which variables to include in your model?

Click to reveal answer

A combination of: (1) Domain knowledge — exposure, country, trade are obvious risk drivers. (2) One-way plots — visual check for signal. (3) AIC improvement — does adding the variable reduce AIC by > 2? (4) Statistical significance — p-value < 0.05. (5) Cross-validation — does it improve out-of-sample Gini? (6) Business sense — can I explain to underwriters why this factor matters? I test 20+ candidate formulas via 20-fold CV and pick the best.

MEDIUM

Q11: What is overdispersion and how do you handle it?

Click to reveal answer

Overdispersion means the actual variance is larger than what the assumed distribution predicts. For Poisson, Var = μ. If actual Var > μ, that's overdispersion. Common in insurance because risks are heterogeneous. Solutions: (1) Quasi-Poisson — adds a dispersion parameter φ so Var = φμ. (2) Negative Binomial — Var = μ + μ²/k. (3) Tweedie — Var = φμp, naturally handles overdispersion via the p parameter. The Tweedie approach is most common in modern pricing.

MEDIUM

Q12: Explain how splines work in a GAM.

Click to reveal answer

A spline is a smooth curve built from basis functions. With k knots, we get roughly k basis functions B₁(x), ..., B_k(x). The smooth is f(x) = γ₁B₁(x) + ... + γ_kB_k(x). The model estimates the weights γᵢ via penalized maximum likelihood. A smoothing parameter λ penalizes the integrated squared second derivative (wiggliness). Higher λ → smoother curve. REML (Restricted Maximum Likelihood) automatically chooses λ. The effective degrees of freedom (EDF) tells you how flexible the resulting smooth is: EDF=1 → straight line, EDF=k-1 → maximum flexibility.

MEDIUM

Q13: What is IBNR and why does it matter for pricing?

Click to reveal answer

IBNR = Incurred But Not Reported. Claims can take months to report and years to settle. Recent years have immature claims that will grow. Development factors estimate the ultimate size: if a 12-month development factor is 1.65, claims at 12 months will grow 65% by ultimate. For pricing, we must use developed (ultimate) claims, not raw incurred. Without development, the model would systematically underprice recent years because their claims haven't fully materialized.

MEDIUM

Q14: How would you compare a GLM vs a GBM for pricing?

Click to reveal answer

Compare on: (1) Predictive accuracy — CV Gini, deviance. GBM often wins slightly. (2) Interpretability — GLM has clear coefficients/relativities; GBM needs SHAP values. (3) Regulatory acceptance — GLMs are standard; GBMs are gaining acceptance. (4) Rating table extraction — direct from GLM; requires approximation for GBM. Industry practice: use GLM/GAM as the primary model for rating tables, use GBM as a benchmark to check if the GLM misses interactions or non-linearities. If GBM is significantly better, investigate what the GLM is missing.

MEDIUM

Q15: What is credibility and when do you apply it?

Click to reveal answer

Credibility blends observed data with a prior (average) based on data volume: Estimate = Z × observed + (1-Z) × prior, where Z = n/(n+K). Small volume → low Z → lean on average. Large volume → high Z → trust the data. Applied when: (1) a country/trade has few claims → credibility-weight toward portfolio average, (2) an individual risk's experience is used for renewal pricing, (3) any time you're estimating parameters from sparse data. In GAMs, shrinkage/penalization achieves similar results automatically.

HARD (10 Questions)

HARD

Q16: Explain the Tweedie power parameter p. What happens at p=1, p=1.5, p=2?

Click to reveal answer

The Tweedie family has Var(Y) = φμp. The power parameter p determines the distribution type:
• p = 0: Normal
• p = 1: Poisson
• 1 < p < 2: Compound Poisson-Gamma (this is insurance pricing territory)
• p = 2: Gamma
• p = 3: Inverse Gaussian
At p = 1.5 (your model), it's a balanced mix of zero-inflated (Poisson-like) and continuous positive (Gamma-like). As p → 1, more zero mass (more zero claims). As p → 2, fewer zeros but heavier right tail. We estimate p from data using profile likelihood.

HARD

Q17: Why do we use REML instead of ML to estimate smoothing parameters?

Click to reveal answer

ML estimation of smoothing parameters is biased downward — it tends to make smooths too wiggly because it doesn't account for the loss of degrees of freedom from estimating the fixed effects (β). REML (Restricted Maximum Likelihood) marginalizes over the fixed effects before optimizing for λ, giving unbiased estimates of the variance/smoothing parameters. This is the same reason REML is preferred over ML for variance components in mixed models. In mgcv, method = "REML" is the default and recommended approach.

HARD

Q18: How would you detect and handle interaction effects in a pricing model?

Click to reveal answer

Detection: (1) Plot residuals by subgroups (e.g., residuals for UK vs AU at different exposure levels). If patterns differ, there's an interaction. (2) Fit a GBM and examine SHAP interaction values. (3) Add explicit interaction terms and check AIC improvement. (4) Use tensor product smooths: te(log10exp, country) or s(log10exp, by=country).
Handling: (1) In GLM: add interaction terms X₁:X₂. (2) In GAM: use s(x, by=factor) for separate smooths per category, or te(x1, x2) for smooth-smooth interactions. (3) Credibility constraint: only include interactions where both main effects have sufficient volume. With 3,600 claims, I'd be cautious — maybe 1-2 key interactions maximum.

HARD

Q19: Explain the bias-variance tradeoff in the context of k (knots) and λ (smoothing).

Click to reveal answer

Bias = model can't capture the true shape (underfitting). Variance = model is too sensitive to noise (overfitting). k (knots) sets the ceiling: small k → high bias (can't be flexible enough), large k → low bias but high variance potential. λ (smoothing) controls actual flexibility within that ceiling: large λ → high bias (forces straightness), small λ → low bias but high variance (too wiggly). The optimal point minimizes total error = bias² + variance. REML chooses λ to balance this. The modeller sets k large enough that the true shape can be captured, then trusts REML to prevent overfitting via λ. For insurance with moderate data, k=5-10 with REML-selected λ typically hits the sweet spot.

HARD

Q20: How would you build a pricing model for a completely new line of business with no historical data?

Click to reveal answer

(1) Benchmark: Find similar lines (e.g., if pricing cyber, look at E&O/professional liability as a starting point). (2) Industry data: Use ISO/market study data for base rates. (3) Expert judgment: Gather underwriter opinion on key risk factors, translate to relativities. (4) Analogue models: Build a model on the closest existing line and adjust coefficients based on expected differences. (5) Pricing formula: Start with a simple burn-cost model (expected loss / expected premium) with manual adjustments. (6) Monitor aggressively: Review A/E monthly in the first year. Refit quarterly as data accumulates. (7) Credibility ramp: Start with low credibility on own data, gradually increase as volume builds.

HARD

Q21: What is the difference between AIC, BIC, and deviance for model selection?

Click to reveal answer

Deviance = 2 × (log-lik(saturated) − log-lik(model)). Measures fit only. Lower = better fit. Always decreases with more parameters.
AIC = Deviance + 2 × (number of parameters). Penalizes complexity. Balances fit and parsimony. Prefers the model that generalizes best to new data.
BIC = Deviance + log(n) × (number of parameters). Penalty grows with sample size. More conservative than AIC — prefers simpler models, especially with large n.
In practice: AIC is most common in insurance pricing. BIC is used when you want fewer variables. Deviance alone is used in nested model comparisons (deviance tests). For GAMs, AIC needs adjustment for EDF; use the AIC reported by mgcv which accounts for this.

HARD

Q22: How would you handle inflation trends in a multi-year pricing model?

Click to reveal answer

(1) Exposure inflation: Inflate all exposures to the current year using CPI or construction cost index. This makes exposures comparable across years. (2) Claims inflation: Index historical claims to current dollars using claims inflation (often different from CPI — "social inflation" in liability). (3) Superimposed inflation: Beyond economic inflation, liability claims may increase due to legal trends, regulatory changes, etc. Add a trend factor. (4) Year effect in model: Include underwriting year as a variable (or smooth s(uw_year)) to capture residual trends. (5) Projection: When setting rates, project inflation forward to the midpoint of the policy period. (6) Validation: Check A/E by year — if recent years are consistently > 1.0, you're under-adjusting for inflation.

HARD

Q23: What are the key assumptions of a GLM, and how do you check each one?

Click to reveal answer

(1) Distribution assumption: The response follows the specified distribution (e.g., Tweedie). Check: Q-Q plot of deviance residuals; compare deviance statistics across families. (2) Link function assumption: g(μ) = η is the correct transformation. Check: plot residuals vs fitted values — random scatter means OK. (3) Linearity (on link scale): Each predictor has a linear effect on η. Check: plot residuals vs each predictor — curves mean you need a GAM. (4) Independence: Observations are independent. Check: no autocorrelation in residuals; no clustering. (5) Variance function: Var(Y) = φV(μ) holds. Check: plot |residuals| vs fitted — should match assumed V(μ). (6) No multicollinearity: Predictors aren't perfectly correlated. Check: VIF < 5.

HARD

Q24: How would you use a GBM to improve a GLM/GAM model?

Click to reveal answer

(1) Fit GBM on same data and compare CV Gini. If GBM is significantly better → the GLM/GAM is missing something. (2) Check GBM variable importance → any important variables that the GLM excluded? Add them. (3) Check GBM SHAP partial dependence plots → any non-linear shapes the GAM's splines missed? Increase k. (4) Check GBM SHAP interaction values → any interactions the GLM doesn't have? Add them. (5) Use GBM predictions as a "benchmark" premium — compare GLM vs GBM premium for each policy. Large discrepancies flag risks where the GLM may be wrong. (6) Stacking: Use GBM predictions as an additional input feature in the GLM (with care for overfitting).
