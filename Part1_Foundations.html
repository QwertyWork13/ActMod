Chapter 1: What Is Insurance?

Imagine 1,000 families each put $1,000 into a big jar every year. Most years, 990 families don't need anything. But 10 families have house fires and need $80,000 each to fix. The jar has $1,000,000, and fires cost $800,000 — so there's enough to pay everyone! That's insurance. Everyone pays a little so nobody faces a catastrophe alone.

The Core Concept: Risk Pooling

Insurance works because of a mathematical miracle: the Law of Large Numbers.

As the number of independent risks (n) → ∞, the average loss converges to the expected loss.
In math: (1/n) × Σ Xᵢ → E[X] as n → ∞
If you flip a coin 10 times, you might get 7 heads (70%). If you flip it 10,000 times, you'll get very close to 50%. Insurance works the same way — with enough policies, the actual losses become very predictable.

How Does an Insurer Make Money?

Collect Premium$100M from customers
→
Pay Claims$65M to policyholders
→
Pay Expenses$25M operations
→
Profit$10M (10% margin)
The Combined Ratio = (Claims + Expenses) ÷ Premium.
If Combined Ratio < 100% → you make money from underwriting.
If Combined Ratio > 100% → you lose money from underwriting (but might still profit from investing the premiums).

General Liability (GL) — Your World

General Liability covers when your policyholder's business causes injury or damage to a THIRD PARTY.

Example: A construction company is building an office tower. A crane drops materials, injuring a pedestrian. The pedestrian sues for $2M. The construction company's GL policy pays for the legal defense + settlement. This is what you price at QBE.

What makes GL special?

  • Long-tailed: A claim from 2018 might not be fully settled until 2025
  • Hard to predict: Legal costs vary wildly by country/jurisdiction
  • Low frequency, high severity: Most companies have 0 claims, but when they do, they can be enormous
  • Exposure measured by revenue/payroll: Bigger company = more risk

Chapter 2: The Insurance Pricing Pipeline

Pricing is like being a chef. You get raw ingredients (data), follow a recipe (model), and produce a dish (premium). But unlike cooking, you can't taste-test — you won't know if the price was right for years until all the claims come in.

The Complete Pricing Pipeline

The Pricing Formula: Technical Premium

Technical Premium = Pure Premium ÷ (1 - Expense Ratio - Profit Margin)

Where:
Pure Premium = Frequency × Severity
Expense Ratio = Costs / Premium (typically 25-35%)
Profit Margin = Target return (typically 5-15%)
If you expect $650 in claims per customer, your expenses are 25% of premium, and you want 10% profit, then:
Premium = $650 ÷ (1 - 0.25 - 0.10) = $650 ÷ 0.65 = $1,000

What a Pricing Actuary Does vs What a Model Does

🤖 The Model Does:

  • Finds patterns in historical data
  • Estimates base rates and relativities
  • Quantifies how much each factor affects the price
  • Predicts expected losses for new risks

👨‍💼 The Actuary Does:

  • Chooses which data to use
  • Decides the model structure
  • Applies judgment where data is thin
  • Adjusts for trends, inflation, legal changes
  • Communicates results to underwriters

Key Data Concepts

Exposure is like the size of the target. A company with $100M revenue has a bigger "target" for lawsuits than one with $1M. Exposure tells you how much opportunity there is for something to go wrong.

2. Incurred vs Paid vs Ultimate

For pricing, we use Ultimate losses — the estimated total cost when the claim is finally closed. This accounts for IBNR (Incurred But Not Reported) and future development. Your variable capped_trade_inflated_ult_net_incurred_USD is the ultimate, net of reinsurance, capped for large losses, adjusted for inflation.

3. Inflation and Currency

Claims from 2015 and 2023 aren't comparable at face value because of inflation. Your data uses TradeExposureUSD_Inflated — everything is inflated to a common year in USD.

Always inflate historical claims and exposures to a common year before modelling. Otherwise, older years look artificially cheap and the model thinks things are getting worse when it's just inflation.

Chapter 4: Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

EDA is like being a detective. Before you solve the mystery (build the model), you walk around the crime scene (data) and look for clues. What's common? What's unusual? What patterns do you see?

The 5 Questions EDA Must Answer

  1. What does the response variable look like? (Distribution of claims)
  2. What are the key predictors? (Exposure, country, trade, etc.)
  3. Are there relationships? (Do bigger companies have more claims?)
  4. Are there outliers? (Any $50M claims that will distort the model?)
  5. Is there missing data? (Gaps that need handling)

Question 1: What Do Claims Look Like?

This "spike at zero + long right tail" shape is the defining feature of insurance data. Every modelling decision we make is driven by this shape. It's why we can't use Normal distributions or linear regression.

Question 3: Are There Relationships?

The most important relationship in insurance pricing: exposure vs claims.

Critical EDA Finding: The relationship between exposure and claims is NOT a straight line (even on log scale). It curves — claims increase fast at first, then flatten for very large companies. This is exactly why we need a GAM (smooth curve) instead of a GLM (straight line).

Question 4: Outliers

A single $50M claim in a portfolio with a $17K average can completely distort your model. Always check for extreme values and consider capping (truncating) large losses. Your data already has this done: capped_trade_inflated_ult_net_incurred_USD.

Chapter 5: Probability Refresher — Random Variables & Distributions

A "distribution" is like a recipe for randomness. It tells you: "if I picked a random claim, how likely is it to be $0? $1,000? $100,000?" Different distributions give different recipes — and picking the RIGHT recipe for your data is the whole game.

What Is a Random Variable?

A random variable is a number whose value is determined by chance. Examples:

  • X = number of claims a policy gets this year (could be 0, 1, 2, 3...)
  • Y = total dollar amount of claims (could be $0, $5K, $500K...)

Discrete vs Continuous

🔢 Discrete

Takes specific values (usually whole numbers)

Examples: 0, 1, 2, 3 claims

Tool: PMF (Probability Mass Function)
P(X = k) gives probability of exactly k

Key distributions: Poisson, Binomial, Negative Binomial

📏 Continuous

Takes any value in a range

Examples: $0.01, $1,234.56, $5,000,000

Tool: PDF (Probability Density Function)
P(a < X < b) = ∫ f(x) dx from a to b

Key distributions: Normal, Gamma, Lognormal, Pareto

Key Properties of Every Distribution

PropertyWhat It Tells YouFormula Notation
Mean (μ)The average value — the "centre"E[X]
Variance (σ²)How spread out values are from the meanVar(X) = E[(X-μ)²]
Standard Deviation (σ)Square root of variance — in same units as X√Var(X)
SkewnessIs it lopsided? Positive = right-tail. Insurance data: very positive.E[(X-μ)³] / σ³
ModeThe most common value. Insurance: usually $0argmax f(x)

What "Fitting a Distribution" Means

You have a box of data. You try on different "distribution outfits" (Normal? Poisson? Gamma?) and see which one fits best. "Fitting" means finding the parameter values (like λ or α) that make the distribution match your data as closely as possible.
Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE):
Given data x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ, find the parameters θ that maximize:

L(θ) = f(x₁|θ) × f(x₂|θ) × ... × f(xₙ|θ)

In practice, we maximize the log-likelihood: ℓ(θ) = Σ log f(xᵢ|θ)
MLE is like asking: "Which settings for this distribution would make my actual data MOST LIKELY to have occurred?" It's the mathematical way of finding the best fit.

The Exponential Family — Why It Matters

GLMs only work with distributions from the Exponential Family. Fortunately, all the distributions we need are in this family:

Exponential Family Members Used in Insurance:
✅ Normal   ✅ Poisson   ✅ Gamma   ✅ Inverse Gaussian   ✅ Tweedie

❌ NOT in exponential family: Lognormal, Pareto (these are used separately for ILFs, not inside GLMs)

An exponential family distribution can be written as:

f(y|θ,φ) = a(y,φ) × exp[ (yθ - b(θ)) / φ ]

Where: θ = canonical parameter, φ = dispersion, b(θ) determines the distribution

Don't worry about memorizing this formula. The key point is: because these distributions share this structure, GLMs have a unified way to fit them all.

Chapter 6: The Normal Distribution — And Why It Fails

The Normal distribution is the "default" bell curve you learned in school. It's great for heights, test scores, and measurement errors. It's TERRIBLE for insurance claims. This chapter explains why, so you understand what motivates everything that follows.

The Normal Distribution

f(x) = (1 / σ√2π) × e−(x−μ)²/2σ²

Parameters: μ (mean), σ (standard deviation)
Range: −∞ to +∞     Mean = μ     Variance = σ²

Properties of the Normal Distribution

PropertyValue
ShapePerfectly symmetric bell curve
Range−∞ to +∞ (any real number)
Mean = Median = ModeAll equal to μ (perfectly centred)
68-95-99.7 rule68% within ±1σ, 95% within ±2σ, 99.7% within ±3σ
Skewness0 (no skew)
Varianceσ² (constant — doesn't change with the mean)

Why It Fails for Insurance Claims — 4 Deadly Problems

❌ Problem 1: Negative Values

Normal allows claims < $0. Your data: mean = $17,893, std = $116,000. A Normal fit puts 45% probability below zero!

You can't have a claim of −$50,000.

❌ Problem 2: Spike at Zero

90% of your policies have exactly $0 claims. Normal is continuous — it can't put a big chunk of probability at a single point.

❌ Problem 3: Skewness

Normal is symmetric: mean = median. Your data: mean = $17,893 but median = $0. That's massively right-skewed. Normal can't do skewness.

❌ Problem 4: Constant Variance

Normal assumes Var(Y) = σ² everywhere. But a $100M company has way more variability in claims than a $100K company. Variance grows with the mean in insurance.

The Normal distribution fails on every single property that insurance data has. This is why the first thing you learn in an insurance pricing course is "forget Normal, use Poisson + Gamma + Tweedie."

Chapter 7: The Poisson Distribution — Counting Claims

Counting raindrops hitting your window each minute. Counting how many emails arrive per hour. Counting how many claims a policy gets per year. Whenever you're counting "how many times does something happen?" — that's Poisson.

The Poisson Distribution

P(X = k) = e−λ × λk / k!     for k = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...

Parameter: λ (lambda) = average rate of events
Mean = λ     Variance = λ     (Mean = Variance!)

Why Poisson Works for Claim Counts

Insurance NeedPoisson PropertyMatch?
Claims are 0, 1, 2, 3...Range: 0, 1, 2, 3... (non-negative integers)✅ Perfect
Most policies have 0 claimsWhen λ small: P(0) = e−λ is large✅ When λ=0.1, P(0)=90.5%
Events are independentAssumes independence✅ Roughly true
Rate is constant for similar risksOne parameter λ per group✅ Model estimates different λ for each risk

How λ Changes the Shape

When λ is small (like 0.1 for your construction portfolio), the Poisson distribution puts the vast majority of probability at zero. This is exactly what your data looks like: 90% of policies have zero claims.

Computing Poisson Probabilities — Step by Step

Let's say λ = 0.1 (your data). What's the probability of 0, 1, 2 claims?

P(0 claims) = e−0.1 × 0.1⁰ / 0! = 0.905 × 1 / 1 = 90.5%
P(1 claim) = e−0.1 × 0.1¹ / 1! = 0.905 × 0.1 / 1 = 9.0%
P(2 claims) = e−0.1 × 0.1² / 2! = 0.905 × 0.01 / 2 = 0.45%
P(3+ claims) ≈ 0.02% (very rare!)

See? 90.5% zeros + 9% one-claim + 0.5% two-claims ≈ your real data perfectly!

Mean = Variance: The Equidispersion Property

Poisson assumes Mean = Variance = λ. This is a strict assumption. If your data has Variance > Mean (called overdispersion), you have options:

SituationWhat It MeansSolution
Var = MeanPerfect PoissonUse Poisson ✅
Var > MeanOverdispersion — more variability than Poisson expectsQuasi-Poisson or Negative Binomial
Var < MeanUnderdispersion — less variability (rare)Rarely an issue in insurance
Why does overdispersion happen? Because the "rate" λ isn't truly constant — different policies have different risk levels. A mix of Poissons with different λ's has more variance than a single Poisson. The model's job is to explain this variation using predictors (exposure, country, etc.).

But Poisson Only Answers "How Many?"

Poisson tells us a policy will have 0.1 claims per year. But a claim could cost $1,000 or $1,000,000. For the "how much?" question, we need severity distributions → Next chapter.

Chapter 8: Gamma, Lognormal & Pareto — Claim Sizes

We know how many claims to expect (Poisson). Now: how heavy is each claim? It's like weighing fish — most are small, some are medium, and once in a while you catch a whale. We need a distribution that only gives positive weights and allows for the occasional whale.

The Gamma Distribution

f(x) = (βα / Γ(α)) × xα−1 × e−βx     for x > 0

Parameters: α (shape) and β (rate)
Mean = α/β     Variance = α/β²     Range: (0, ∞)

Key Properties

PropertyValueWhy It Matters
Range(0, ∞) — strictly positiveClaims can't be negative ✅
ShapeRight-skewed when α < 1Most claims small, few large ✅
VarianceVar = α/β² = μ²/αVar ∝ μ² — variance grows with mean ✅
Flexibilityα controls skewness: small α = very skewed, large α = more symmetricYour data: α ≈ 0.49 = very skewed ✅
GLM familyMember of exponential familyWorks directly in GLM ✅

How α Changes the Shape

Your data has α ≈ 0.49, meaning extremely skewed. The median claim ($29K) is much less than the mean ($128K). This extreme skewness is why Gamma is better than Normal for claim sizes.

The Lognormal Distribution

If you take a Normal bell curve and pass every value through the "e to the power of x" machine, the bell curve gets squished and stretched into a right-skewed shape. All negatives become tiny positives. That's Lognormal.
If log(X) ~ Normal(μ, σ²), then X ~ Lognormal(μ, σ²)

Mean = eμ + σ²/2     Variance = (eσ² − 1) × e2μ + σ²     Range: (0, ∞)

The Pareto Distribution

Pareto is the "80/20 rule" distribution. 80% of claims are tiny, but 20% account for most of the money. It has the heaviest tail of all these distributions — meaning it predicts very large claims more often.
f(x) = α × xmα / xα+1     for x ≥ xm

Parameters: α (shape/tail index), xm (minimum value)
Smaller α = heavier tail = more extreme large claims

The Variance Function — A Key Concept for GLMs

Each distribution has a relationship between its mean and variance. This is called the variance function:

DistributionVariance FunctionWhat It Means
NormalVar(Y) = σ² (constant)Variance doesn't depend on mean
PoissonVar(Y) = μVariance = mean
GammaVar(Y) = φμ²Variance grows as square of mean
TweedieVar(Y) = φμpGeneralizes all of the above!
The Tweedie variance function Var(Y) = φμp unifies Normal (p=0), Poisson (p=1), and Gamma (p=2). By setting p=1.5, you get something between Poisson and Gamma — perfect for aggregate insurance losses.
